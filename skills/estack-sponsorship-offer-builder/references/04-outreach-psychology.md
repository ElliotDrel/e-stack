## Cialdini: the 7 principles, tuned for a follow-up sequence

1. **Reciprocity.** People give back after receiving. Works best early, when the relationship is thin — which is exactly where a cold sequence lives. The line between use and abuse: give something of real value with no hidden agenda ("manipulative reciprocity is when the gift has a hidden agenda the recipient would reject if they knew about it"). For sponsorship: audience insights, a relevant intro, a data point about their market — given, not teased.
2. **Commitment & consistency.** Small yeses precede big ones; attitudes follow behavior. Ask for a 15-minute call, not a contract. A sponsor who replies "send me the numbers" has begun committing.
3. **Social proof.** Under uncertainty, people copy comparable others. Name comparable sponsors (with permission) or comparable outcomes. Specific and small beats grand: "two firms in your space took spots last month" outperforms "everyone loves us".
4. **Authority.** Expertise lowers resistance. In outreach this is competence signaling: knowing their business, citing your audience data precisely, writing like someone who has done this before.
5. **Liking.** Similarity and genuine warmth reduce resistance; it signals "this person understands my interests". Personalization that proves you actually looked is the honest form.
6. **Scarcity.** The most powerful urgency lever "and the most dangerous when misused". Deploy only after value is established, and only when literally true (real category exclusivity, real capacity, a real event date). Premature or fake scarcity signals desperation and kills credibility.
7. **Unity.** Shared identity ("as a [city] company", "as fellow alumni") outperforms behavior appeals. Use when a genuine shared identity exists; inventing one reads as pandering.

**Mechanism vs structure (Semalytics):** these principles are the emotional mechanism inside a message, not its outline. Pick the one or two that fit this buyer — proof-driven buyers respond to authority and methodology; risk-averse buyers respond to loss framing and certainty; relationship buyers need warmth before logic. One mechanism per message; stacking all seven into one email reads as a con.

## Blount: why sequences work and how to run them

**The three laws.**
- *Universal Law of Need:* desperation repels — "the more you communicate that you need the sale, the more likely they say no." A full pipeline is what keeps each individual email calm. Never let a follow-up smell like need.
- *30-Day Rule:* prospecting done in any 30-day window pays out over the following 90. Sponsorship translation: outreach starts months before the event, because the sequence you send this month books meetings next quarter.
- *Law of Replacement:* prospects fall out constantly; keep adding new ones rather than squeezing the dead ones harder.

**The three Ps that stall people:** procrastination, perfectionism, paralysis by analysis. The cure is shipping the sequence on schedule, not polishing message four.

**The triple touch.** Alternate channels — email, phone, LinkedIn/social — instead of stacking one. Familiarity compounds across channels ("Law of Familiarity"): the fourth touch lands differently when the name is already known.

**Message rules.** Prospects take meetings for their reasons, not yours. Every touch must be "quick, simple, direct, and relevant", answer "what's in it for me", and make one low-risk ask. The five-step call framework (name → identify yourself → purpose → "because" bridge → direct ask, then silence) ports to email: the "because" bridge — one specific, relevant reason this message is for them — is what separates outreach from spam.

**RBOs (reflex responses, brush-offs, objections).** "Not interested" in the first two seconds is a reflex, not a decision; "send me some info" is usually a test — respond with a narrowing question ("happy to — what specifically would you want it to cover?"). A real objection (price, timing, exclusivity) is engagement: acknowledge, answer, re-ask. Turnaround pattern: anchor (prepared response) → disrupt (agree, take away the fight: "I figured you'd be slammed") → ask again.

## Sequence design rules (synthesis)

- Four touches is a floor, not a ceiling; most replies come from follow-ups, not the opener. Space them days apart, not hours, and vary the angle each time — new value, new proof, new hook — never "just bumping this".
- Each message carries exactly one principle-mechanism and one ask.
- The last message hands them an easy out (a clean no-oriented close). Ending the sequence with grace preserves the relationship for next season — sponsorship is a small world and this year's "no" is next year's renewal prospect.
- Every claim in every email must survive the literal-truth test, because the sequence's job is to open a multi-year relationship, not win one reply.
