## The core rule

**The customer is the hero; the brand is the guide.** "Brands that position themselves as heroes unknowingly compete with their potential customers." Applied here: the sponsor is the hero of the packet, the emails, and the meeting. The org is Yoda, not Luke. The most common sponsorship-packet failure is a document that stars the organization — its history, its mission, its board — with the sponsor cast as wallet. Every page gets re-aimed at the sponsor's story.

Second rule: **clarity over cleverness.** "If you confuse, you lose"; the brain buys what it can digest. The three-question test — a reader should answer *what's on offer, how it improves their situation, and what to do next* within seconds of picking up any page.

## SB7, mapped to a sponsorship sale

1. **Character (the sponsor)** wants something concrete: hires, leads, brand position with a specific audience. Open the story gap between where they are and that outcome. Keep it concrete ("talent leaders who need engineering interns", not "companies that value innovation").
2. **Problem.** Three layers — *external* (they can't reach this audience efficiently), *internal* (how that feels: their channels are noisy, their booth got ignored, they're under pressure to show recruiting results), *philosophical* (why it's just wrong: companies that invest in this community deserve to be the ones its members choose). "Companies sell solutions to external problems, but customers buy solutions to internal problems." Name a villain if there is a true one — the noise, the job-board slush pile, the interchangeable career fair — root cause, singular, real, no fearmongering.
3. **Guide = empathy + authority.** One line that shows you understand their world, then proof you can deliver: audience data, past sponsor outcomes, testimonials, fulfillment reports. "Customers won't know you care until you tell them."
4. **Plan.** Give them a named, ≤4-step path ("1. Discovery call. 2. We build your package. 3. We run everything. 4. You get the report."). A plan reduces perceived risk and effort — it is the value equation's bottom half in narrative form. Agreement-plan elements (guarantees, commitments) live here too.
5. **Call to action.** Direct ("book the call") plus transitional for the not-ready (audience report, last year's fulfillment report). One obvious button; every packet page and email ends with exactly one next step.
6. **Failure stakes.** What inaction costs — another season of the competitor's logo on this audience, another intern class hired from the same generic pool. Understate rather than dramatize; stakes create urgency only when believable.
7. **Success.** Paint the after-state in their metrics: the hires made, the pipeline filled, how the win looks to *their* boss. "Never assume people understand how your brand can change their lives. Tell them."

## The one-liner

Template: **"We help [character] who struggle with [problem] by [plan], so they can [success]."** Example shape: "We help companies that struggle to reach student founders become the first brand those founders build with, through a season-long partnership we run end to end." Every sponsorship property should have one; it opens the packet, the email, and the answer to "so what do you do?"

## Applying it to the packet's pages

Hero section: value statement + direct CTA above the fold. Problem framing before org description. Guide section: empathy line + authority proof (logos, stats). Plan: 3 steps, visually simple. Success: tangible outcomes. Risk reversal near the ask. Transitional CTA for the not-ready. Minimal copy throughout — Miller's website guidance (aim for few sentences, one obvious action) applies to each packet page. Emphasis shifts by reader stage: early readers need Problem and Success; evaluators need Plan and Authority; late-stage needs risk reversal and proof.
