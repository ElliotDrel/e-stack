## StoryBrand: two rules and seven beats

**The customer is the hero; the brand is the guide.** "Brands that position themselves as heroes unknowingly compete with their potential customers." The most common packet failure is a document starring the org — history, mission, board — with the sponsor cast as wallet. Every page gets re-aimed at the sponsor's story.

**Clarity over cleverness.** "If you confuse, you lose." The three-question test: within seconds of any page, a reader can answer *what's on offer, how it improves their situation, what to do next.*

SB7 mapped to a sponsorship sale:

1. **Character (the sponsor)** wants something concrete: hires, leads, brand position with a specific audience. Keep it concrete ("talent leaders who need engineering interns").
2. **Problem**, three layers — *external* (can't reach this audience efficiently), *internal* (how that feels: noisy channels, ignored booth, pressure to show results), *philosophical* (companies that invest in this community deserve to be the ones it chooses). "Companies sell solutions to external problems, but customers buy solutions to internal problems." Name a villain only if it's real, singular, and root-cause (the job-board slush pile, the interchangeable career fair).
3. **Guide = empathy + authority.** One line showing you understand their world, then proof: audience data, past sponsor outcomes, testimonials.
4. **Plan.** A named path of ≤4 steps ("Discovery call → we build your package → we run everything → you get the report"). A plan is the value equation's bottom half in narrative form — it cuts perceived risk and effort. Guarantees live here.
5. **Call to action.** One direct CTA (book the call) plus one transitional CTA for the not-ready (the audience report). Every page and email ends with exactly one next step.
6. **Failure stakes.** What inaction costs — the competitor's logo on this audience for another season. Understate; stakes only work when believable.
7. **Success.** The after-state in *their* metrics, including how the win looks to their boss. "Never assume people understand how your brand can change their lives. Tell them."

**The one-liner:** "We help [character] who struggle with [problem] by [plan], so they can [success]." It opens the packet, the email, and the answer to "what do you do?"

## Sinek: the golden circle

**Why → How → What**, communicated inside out. Everyone knows what they do; few can state why — the belief, never "to make a profit" (that's a result). The Apple demonstration: the same facts, reversed order ("Everything we do, we believe in challenging the status quo... We just happen to make great computers"), entirely different pull. **"People don't buy what you do, they buy why you do it — and what you do simply proves what you believe."** Decisions are made by the limbic brain, which has no language: features inform, belief moves.

Sponsorship translation:

- **The org's why opens the story.** "We are a student org founded in..." is outside-in. Start with the belief, then the how, then the event as *proof* of the belief.
- **Belief-fit is a sponsor filter.** "The goal is to do business with people who believe what you believe." Sponsors who share the why renew and activate; sponsors buying only reach are transactions. Ask in discovery what the company believes about this audience.
- **Why-first cold emails.** One line of belief can carry a hook where a feature list dies, and it self-selects for the replies worth having.
- **Limits.** Why-language without proof is a poster. The golden circle sets the message's *order*; the value equation and audience data still have to cash the check.
