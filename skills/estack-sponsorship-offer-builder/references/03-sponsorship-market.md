## The core truths

1. **Sponsorship is about the audience.** Sponsors buy access to a specific group of people and want that group to take a specific action. Everything in the sale flows from audience data, not from how good the event is.
2. **Proposals don't sell sponsorship, people do.** The document confirms a conversation. Never send a proposal cold, never attach one to a cold email (Baylis: "by far, the biggest mistake you can make" — mass attachments get reported as spam), and never write one before discovery.
3. **Who you ask matters more than how you ask.** Right contacts: Brand, Marketing, Sponsorship, Business Development, Communications, Product; CSR for philanthropy. Build the prospect list from audience overlap: which companies want your people, and who sponsors your competitors (and who competes with *those* sponsors — "if one bank has interest in your brand, I bet they all do").
4. **The sale is 20% of the work.** Activation and fulfillment are the other 80%, and renewal — the profitable part — is won there.

## Why not gold/silver/bronze

Sponsors resent pre-bundled tiers: they force buying things they don't want and signal "we couldn't be bothered to learn what you need" (Baylis calls tier pricing "lazy, cookie-cutter"). When premium tiers do sell, it's often for reasons unrelated to the listed benefits — Baylis' 2008 example: a company held $250K for hospital-gala "Gold" status purely to protect its relationship with a hospital it sold X-ray machines to. Copying visible tier structures copies the survivorship, not the mechanism. The alternative: build a broad **asset inventory**, value each asset, and assemble a custom package per sponsor from discovery. (Presenting the custom package as three options is the Enns layer; see the positioning-and-pricing reference.)

## The five stages

1. **Inventory & valuation.** List everything sellable; assign each a defensible market value; document the methodology because sponsors ask. Low-value assets get cut. "No assets, no sales."
2. **Prospecting.** Warm paths first: board/committee members treated like title sponsors, advice visits, referrals from current sponsors and vendors, LinkedIn concentric circles.
3. **The first meeting: discovery ONLY.** Bring a short question list and a notebook. Do not bring packages, pricing sheets, or tiers. The only goals: learn, and **BAMFAM** — book a meeting from a meeting, so the next step is locked before leaving the room. Unresponsive prospects: a defined cadence (roughly one touch per day over seven days, alternating phone and email, then move to the next contact at that company) rather than open-ended nagging.
4. **The proposal.** Written after discovery, customized without *saying* "customized" (announcing it reads needy), maximum six pages, no tier names, and only when the prospect asks for it.
5. **Activation, fulfillment, renewal.** Deliver everything, document everything, then use the fulfillment meeting — while the sponsor is looking at their own ROI — to ask about next year.

## The 6-page proposal template

1. **Title page** — org logo, opportunity name, tagline. Never call it a "sponsorship package".
2. **Audience** — demographics, segments, engagement data. This page comes before anything about the org, because it's what the sponsor is buying.
3. **The opportunity** — one paragraph on the org/cause (no sob story), one on the program/event.
4. **Menu, not levels** — action-framed asset groups ("Recruit early-career engineers", "Get product into the hands of X"), opened with a working-philosophy statement inviting the sponsor's own activation ideas.
5. **Sample activations** — concrete ideas showing sophistication (branding, sampling, experience, contests). Sponsors rarely buy these as-is; the page exists to make them think.
6. **Contact page** — warm call to action, framed as "this document is the start of a conversation". No tear-off payment form.

## Discovery questions (working set)

The full bank is ~40 questions in four groups; the highest-leverage ones:

*Audience:* Who is your ideal customer? Is [our audience] a segment you try to engage, and how? What action do you want them to take after engaging with you? What audience data matters in your sponsorship decisions?

*Business goals:* How do you move people into your sales funnel, and what's the first action they take? What does a partnership need to produce to be a success? I noticed you sponsor X — what do you like most about that partnership? What does it cost you to acquire a customer?

*Success measures:* What's working and not working in sponsorships you run today? How important is employee engagement? Networking — who do you hope to meet? What indicators do you use to judge a sponsorship investment? Any must-have benefits? What frustrations with other properties could we avoid?

*Protocol:* ask permission before notes/recording, summarize answers back, listen for emotional cues and repeated phrases (they mark the real values), and tag insights by priority.

## The 10 proposal mistakes

Skipping sponsor research; targeting misaligned sponsors; generic value claims instead of measurable outcomes; typos/unprofessionalism (sponsors read presentation quality as delivery quality); ignoring legal/ethical fit; treating the deal as transactional instead of a relationship; no post-event follow-up; generic tiers; no performance tracking; underselling the property.

## Fulfillment: where renewals are won

**Report structure (5 pages):** title; opportunity overview with updated audience data ("25% of attendees are accountants earning $78k/year" beats "diverse professional audience"); the **deliverables chart** — every promised asset, status (delivered / undelivered / overdelivered), notes, amount charged, actual value delivered ("spent $10k, received $25k in value" is ROI made visible); photo/screenshot proof; thank-you plus improvement conversation. Produce it fast — within days of the event (Baylis says within 24 hours; HeySummit allows up to two weeks but warns against staleness).

**Metric integrity (HeySummit):** registrations show demand, not sponsor awareness; attendance proves reach, not lead quality; clicks show engagement, not revenue. "Do not turn weak tracking into confident ROI language." Claim revenue only with real downstream tracking; report shortfalls transparently with a make-good. During the event, run a deliverables tracker: promise / proof to capture / owner / status — proof that isn't captured live can't be reported later.
