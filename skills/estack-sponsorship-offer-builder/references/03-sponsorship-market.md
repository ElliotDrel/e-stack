## The core truths

1. **Sponsorship is about the audience.** Sponsors buy access to a specific group and want that group to take a specific action. Everything flows from audience data, not from how good the event is.
2. **Proposals don't sell sponsorship, people do.** The document confirms a conversation. Never send one cold, never attach one to a cold email (Baylis: "by far, the biggest mistake you can make"), never write one before discovery, and wait until the prospect asks for it.
3. **Who you ask matters more than how.** Right contacts: Brand, Marketing, Sponsorship, BD, Communications, Product; CSR for philanthropy. Build the list from audience overlap: who wants your people, who sponsors comparable properties, and who competes with *those* sponsors ("if one bank has interest in your brand, I bet they all do").
4. **The sale is 20% of the work.** Activation and fulfillment are the other 80%, and renewal — the profitable part — is won there. Sponsors who don't get what they paid for ask for refunds and tell colleagues.

## Why not gold/silver/bronze

Pre-bundled tiers force sponsors to buy things they don't want and signal "we couldn't be bothered to learn what you need" ("lazy, cookie-cutter", leaves money on the table). When premium tiers do sell, it's often for unrelated business reasons — Baylis' example: a company held $250K for hospital-gala "Gold" status purely to protect its X-ray-machine sales relationship with that hospital. Copying tier structures copies the survivorship, not the mechanism. The alternative: build a broad **asset inventory**, value each asset defensibly (document the methodology — sponsors ask), and assemble a custom package per sponsor from discovery. Presenting it as three options is the Enns layer (reference 02).

## The five stages

1. **Inventory & valuation.** List everything sellable; value it; cut low-value assets. "No assets, no sales."
2. **Prospecting.** Warm paths first: board members treated like title sponsors, advice visits, referrals from current sponsors and vendors, LinkedIn concentric circles.
3. **First meeting: discovery ONLY.** Bring a question list and a notebook — no packages, no pricing sheets. Goals: learn, and **BAMFAM** (book a meeting from a meeting — the next step locked before leaving the room). Unresponsive prospects: about one touch per day for seven days, alternating phone and email, then move to the next contact at that company.
4. **The proposal.** Written after discovery, customized without announcing "customized" (it reads needy), max six pages, no tiers, sent when asked for.
5. **Activation, fulfillment, renewal.** Deliver everything, document everything, then use the fulfillment meeting — while the sponsor is looking at their own ROI — to ask about next year.

Common packet mistakes beyond the above: generic value claims instead of measurable outcomes; typos and sloppiness (sponsors read presentation quality as delivery quality); no performance tracking; underselling the property.

## The 6-page proposal template

1. **Title page** — org logo, opportunity name, tagline. Never "sponsorship package".
2. **Audience** — before anything about the org, because it's what the sponsor is buying: demographics, segments, engagement data.
3. **The opportunity** — one paragraph on the org/cause (no sob story), one on the program/event.
4. **Menu, not levels** — action-framed asset groups ("Recruit early-career engineers"), opened with a working-philosophy statement inviting the sponsor's own activation ideas.
5. **Sample activations** — concrete ideas (branding, sampling, experience, contests) that show sophistication; sponsors rarely buy them as-is, but they start the thinking.
6. **Contact page** — warm CTA, framed as the start of a conversation. No tear-off payment form.

## Discovery questions (working set)

The full bank is ~40 questions in four groups; the highest-leverage:

*Audience:* Who is your ideal customer? Is [our audience] a segment you try to engage, and how? What action do you want them to take after engaging with you? What audience data matters in your sponsorship decisions?

*Business goals:* How do you move people into your sales funnel, and what's the first action they take? What does a partnership need to produce to be a success? I noticed you sponsor X — what do you like most about that partnership? What does it cost you to acquire a customer?

*Success measures:* What's working and not working in sponsorships you run today? How important is employee engagement? Networking — who do you hope to meet? What indicators do you use to judge a sponsorship investment? Any must-have benefits? What frustrations with other properties could we avoid?

*Protocol:* ask permission before notes/recording, summarize answers back, listen for emotional cues and repeated phrases (they mark the real values).

## Fulfillment: where renewals are won

**Report structure (5 pages):** title; opportunity overview with updated audience data ("25% of attendees are accountants earning $78k/year" beats "diverse professional audience"); the **deliverables chart** — every promised asset: status (delivered / undelivered / overdelivered), notes, amount charged, actual value delivered ("spent $10k, received $25k in value" is ROI made visible); photo/screenshot proof; thank-you plus improvement conversation. Produce it within days of the event.

**Metric integrity (HeySummit):** registrations show demand, not sponsor awareness; attendance proves reach, not lead quality; clicks show engagement, not revenue. "Do not turn weak tracking into confident ROI language." Claim revenue only with real downstream tracking; report shortfalls transparently with a make-good. During the event, run a deliverables tracker (promise / proof to capture / owner / status) — proof not captured live can't be reported later.
