## The situation this call sits in

The sponsor replied to a cold email and booked a call. They are curious, not sold. The temptation is to pitch, because interest feels like permission. The research is unanimous that this is the error: run discovery first, present only against discovered needs, and close on a concrete next step. The Sponsorship Collective adds the hard version for first meetings that were cold-sourced: discovery ONLY, no packages in the room. For a call booked from an email that already sketched the offer, a short tailored presentation is fine — but only after discovery, and only the parts that answer what discovery surfaced.

## Opening: the agenda contract

Sandler's upfront contract, five parts: objective, your agenda, their agenda, timeline, outcome. 30MPC's PPO compresses it:

- **Purpose:** "The goal of this call is to understand what you're trying to get in front of [audience] for, and see whether what we run actually supports it."
- **Plan:** "I'd love to ask about how you approach this today; then I'll share how the partnership works, even if that's just five minutes at the end."
- **Outcome:** "By the end, let's have a frank read on whether this is a fit. Anything you want to make sure we cover?"

An agenda stated out loud relaxes the room (they know there's no ambush) and licenses you to ask real questions.

## Diagnosis: situation → problem → impact

Talk ratio: the best discovery calls run under ~46% seller talk time. Prepare around a dozen questions; ask the ones the conversation earns.

- **Situation:** "What prompted you to take this call?" (30MPC notes nobody takes every cold meeting — the reason they took *this one* is the thread to pull.)
- **Problem, via "typically":** "Typically when a company wants in front of [audience], it's either pipeline — hiring or leads — or brand positioning with that group. Which is closer for you?" Offering the two typical shapes makes it easy for them to self-identify and shows pattern knowledge.
- **Impact:** "When did this become a priority?" "What's it costing to leave unsolved?" "Who else feels it?" Dig past the surface answer (Sandler: don't accept the first emotive answer; find the root) — the difference between "we want brand awareness" and "we missed our intern class target two years running" is the difference between a logo sale and a real partnership.

**The parallel story.** When they name a problem, answer with a two-sentence story of a comparable sponsor who had it and what happened. Proof through pattern, and it lets them sell themselves.

## Presenting: match, then stop

Present only the two or three offer components that answer discovered needs, framed in their words and their metrics. Cardone's bridge questions, asked at each stage boundary: **"Have you seen enough to make a decision?"** / "What do you like about what you've seen so far?" / "What problems do you see us helping with?" Buyers frequently decide before the seller finishes; continuing to present past the decision point un-sells. When the bridge question says they're done, stop and close.

## Closing: the Five Minute Drill

Reserve the last five minutes, always:

1. **Confirm intent:** "So — what do you think?" If hedged: "Before we talk next steps — knowing what you know now, is this worth more of your time?" A real no now beats a slow no over six emails.
2. **Timeline:** anchor to their real dates (event date, budget cycle, hiring season): "When would this need to be live to matter for you?"
3. **Suggest the next step, don't ask for one:** "Normally from here we'd put together three options based on what you told me and walk through them next week — does Tuesday work?" Book the meeting in the meeting (BAMFAM). A call that ends in "I'll send some stuff over" was a loss.

## After

Recap email within 24 hours: their goals in their words, what you'll bring next time, the booked date. The proposal that follows is built from this call's notes — which is the whole reason the call came before the proposal.
