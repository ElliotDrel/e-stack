## Situation playbooks

Each situation shifts which tactics carry the weight. Pick the hook and proof that fit.

### Fundraising / investors
The chosen feeling and quiet demand do the work. Investors pattern-match on momentum and scarcity, so prove traction with small specifics, not adjectives, and make the round feel like something they're being let into.
- Hook options: a recent post or thesis of theirs, a portfolio overlap, a mutual founder/investor, a milestone you just hit.
- Proof: "we just crossed [specific number] with [specific customer type]." "two funds you'd know are already in."
- Ask: weightless. "want the 2-min version of what we're building? happy to drop it here." Not "can we hop on a call."
- Avoid: spraying your deck, leading with your raise size, sounding like every other founder in their inbox.

### Hiring / recruiting
You're selling the mission and the person's specific fit, not listing perks. The chosen feeling is everything: great people don't reply to blasts, they reply when it's obviously about them.
- Hook: their work, a project they shipped, a post, a repo, a mutual.
- Proof (of the opportunity, not bragging): "still tiny, building [the thing], and your name came up fast when I asked who actually gets [domain]."
- Ask: weightless. "mind if I show you what we're building? two minutes, no pitch." Not "can you do a 45-min screen."
- Avoid: reciting the job description, "we're a rocketship," generic "your background is impressive."

### Partnerships / BD
Lead with the specific overlap and the value to them. Connect a real detail about their product or audience to why a partnership is obviously mutual.
- Hook: a concrete overlap. "your users and ours are basically the same people doing [activity]."
- Proof: "we did a similar thing with [comparable partner] and it drove [specific small result]."
- Ask: weightless and exploratory. "worth a quick look at what a collab could be? I can sketch it in three bullets, no meeting yet."
- Avoid: a deck before a hello, "synergies," asking for a sponsorship/integration in line one.

### Press, podcasts, speaking
The single best cold message in the source is a speaking pitch (see annotated example below). Make it about their audience and pre-empt the "this will be boring/salesy" objection out loud.
- Hook: genuine familiarity with their show/publication, a specific episode or piece.
- Objection named first: "I promise this won't be a lame product pitch."
- Proof: "the last one I did sold out / got [specific reaction]."
- Ask: weightless and flexible. "dates and times don't matter, we'll work around you."
- Avoid: a press release, "I'd be a great guest," making them do the framing work.

### Job-seeking / landing a role at a company you'd work for
You can email any company you'd like to work for, full stop. No posted job, "we only hire in [city]," "must have [degree]," none of it disqualifies you, because companies (especially startups) make exceptions for the right person who reaches out well. So the email is not an application, it's an introduction: who you are, why you're reaching out, why they should care. One caveat worth being honest with the user about: cold email lands jobs far more reliably when the sender is actually competent at something valuable and can point to having done it. If they can't, the fix is the proof, not the wording.

This situation leans hardest on specific, laddered competence proof and the weightless ask. Skip the chosen-feeling theatrics ("you're one of 10") that fit a launch, because here they read false. Just be a real person who'd be useful.

- **Format (the spine of the email):** who you are, one line. Why you're reaching out, one line. Why they should care, the proof. Then the ask.
- **Hook:** something true about them or the company, no fabrication. "saw you just raised a seed round and want to help you build the product." It is completely fine to admit you found them because they raised, that's honest, and honest beats a fake "fan for years."
- **Proof:** two or three bullets of relevant work, each laddered to a hard specific. "built [feature] at [company], a big driver of ARR." "shipped [side project], 100k active users." Pick the two most relevant, not your whole résumé.
- **Ask:** one specific next step, weightless. "worth a chat?" beats "are you hiring?" or a vague "let me know if there's a fit." Give them one clear thing to reply to.
- **Length:** more room than a DM but still tight. Ceiling ~200 words; the strongest job emails run well under that (three bullets and a sign-off). Cut buzzwords first, then cut again.
- **It's a numbers game.** Even a great email to the right person often gets no reply, for reasons outside the sender's control (bad timing, not a priority, lost in the inbox, spam folder). Tell the user this plainly: one email is not a strategy. The advice is to do this for 10, 20, 30 companies they'd genuinely want to work at, follow up once each, and expect a few to convert. Volume across good targets, not one perfect shot.
- **Finding the person and the address:** email the person who'd actually hire for the role, the hiring manager, or the CEO if it's a small company or you're unsure. Addresses are usually `firstname@company.com` (guessable) or findable via tools like Nymeria, ContactOut, or Clay.
- **Avoid:** waiting for a posted job, a life-story email, "executed on key initiatives" buzzword soup, no ask, more than one follow-up.

## The seven ready-to-send templates

### 1. You've launched and have proof
```
[firstname] :)
we just crossed [milestone] with teams like yours, and your name keeps coming up as exactly who we built this for.
mind if I show you? two minutes, no pitch.
[name] (previously [credential])
```

### 2. You have no proof yet
```
[firstname] :)
bit of a long shot. I'm building [one line on what it does], and you're one of maybe five people whose honest take I'd actually trust.
can I show you what I've got?
[name]
```

### 3. They liked or commented on your post
```
hey [firstname], saw your comment on my post about [topic]. [one real reaction to it]. building something around exactly that. want an early look?
```

### 4. A mutual connection sent you
```
hey [firstname], [mutual] said you're the person who actually gets [topic]. I'm working on [one line] and would love your eyes on it for two minutes. open to it?
```

### 5. You're not sure they're the decision-maker
```
hey [firstname], you might not be the right person for this, and no worries if so. who on your team would actually care about [outcome]? happy to take it to them and leave you alone :)
```

### 6. They gave you a soft no
```
totally fair, [firstname], I won't chase you. want me to send over [the one genuinely useful thing] so it's there when the timing's better?
```

### 7. A thread went cold months ago
```
hey [firstname], this got buried back in [month], that's on me. still think [the thing] is worth your two minutes. want me to resend the short version?
```

## The ghost follow-up sequence

Most people won't reply to the first message. The follow-up decides everything. Never send "just bumping this." The trick across all of these is to hand them an easy no, which makes them stop bracing and actually answer. Space them a few days apart. Never beg, every message gives them an exit.

**Follow-up 1 (the workhorse):**
```
hey [name],
just checking in, any thoughts on [the thing I mentioned]?
wanted to give you first dibs.
no worries if it's not the right fit right now.
[name]
```
That last line is the whole trick. It looks far too soft to do anything, and it's the line that revives written-off deals.

**Follow-up 2:**
```
hey [firstname], following up once more. you're on a short list I put together myself, so I didn't want this to slip away. no worries if it's not a fit right now :)
```

**Follow-up 3 (last touch):**
```
hey [firstname], last note from me. we're keeping early access to about 10 people before we announce, and there are a couple of spots left. [smallest possible ask] whenever suits you.
```

## The best cold message ever received (annotated)

A founder pitched the author a speaking slot. He said yes before finishing it. It used a subject line, but a lowercase, offhand one that reads like a text, not an ad. That's the only kind of subject that works on an InMail.

Subject (lowercase, offhand): `i promise if you do this speaking engagement it won't be lame or boring`

Body, paraphrased so you see the moves:
- Opens by naming the objection out loud (it might be boring for you) before he can think it. **Tactic: say the objection first.**
- Pitches a thesis that complements his, so it's about him and his audience. **Tactic: make it about them.**
- "the first one sold out, haven't even announced the next and it's already going quick." **Tactic: prove demand without bragging, small and specific.**
- "I'm a big fan of your newsletter." **Tactic: a real, specific detail, not "love what you do."**
- "p.s. dates and times don't matter, we can schedule around you." **Tactic: weightless ask.**

Every move stacks toward one feeling: this was written for me, by a person, and saying yes costs me nothing. That's the target for every message this skill produces.

## Job-seeking example emails (annotated)

These are real (or lightly redacted) emails that got replies and landed work. None references a posted job. All three run the same spine, who you are, why you're reaching out, why they should care, and all stay short. Use them as proof of the shape, not as scripts to send raw.

**1. The high-schooler to a CEO (got a reply from Snapchat's founder).** Opens by respecting his time, then three bullets, then a one-word ask:
- "I'll only write three bullet points."
- programming since 8th grade / most experience in Java, Obj-C, Android, iOS / want to intern this summer as a high-school junior.
- the entire ask: "How?"

What it does: zero throat-clearing, hard specifics over adjectives, and an ask so small it costs nothing to answer. **Tactics: keep it short, ladder claims to specifics, weightless ask.**

**2. The copywriter to a founder.** Opens on the product, not himself, then asks one question:
- "[product] is so good. used it last week for [reason]" is a real, specific detail, not "love what you're building."
- "how are you doing on copywriting at the moment?" is one clear question.
- "asking because I write for startups" tells him who you are in one line, placed after the hook.
- "don't want to blast you with a wall of text" names the objection and points to the work rather than dumping it.

**Tactics: open about them, one ask, say the objection first, don't fabricate the compliment (it's a true detail).**

**3. The builder to a seed-stage founder (hypothetical, modeled on a real one).** Honest about how he found them, two relevant bullets, soft ask:
- "heard about [company] from a friend... saw you just raised a seed round and I'd like to help you build the product" is an honest hook, no fake "fan for years."
- worked at [company] on [feature], a big ARR driver / built [project], 100k active users: two laddered proof bullets, picked for relevance.
- "can share more, but these two feel most relevant... worth a chat?" is one weightless ask.

**Tactics: honest hook, laddered proof, pick the two most relevant not the whole CV, one weightless ask.**

The throughline: none of these is clever or perfect. Anyone could write them. They just avoid the anti-patterns and follow the spine. That's the bar, not artistry, just a real human being specific and easy to say yes to.
