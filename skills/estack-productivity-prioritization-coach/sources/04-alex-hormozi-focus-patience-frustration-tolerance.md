## What this source contributes to the skill

The **persistence layer** the rest of the skill was missing. RPM, the five filters, and the momentum engine are all machinery for *choosing correctly*. This source is about *not switching off a choice already made*, which is a different skill and fails for different reasons.

It also supplies three usable in-the-moment decision questions and the "known hard vs unknown hard" test for new opportunities.

## Synthesized takeaways

### The three traits

Hormozi's claim: there are three non-technical reasons entrepreneurs fail, and they are traits, not tactics.

> "When I talk to entrepreneurs, they always seek the tactics... They lack tactics, no. You do not lack tactics, you lack character."

The three:

1. **Focus** — "you have to be able to do one thing." His definition is stronger than concentration. Focus is refusing attractive alternatives: "We need to not do new stuff. We need to keep doing the old stuff. We need to not pursue shiny objects. That's what focus is."
2. **Patience** — "you have to do it for a long period of time." Willingness to "work now, get no reward, and hope that we will get something later."
3. **Frustration tolerance** — willingness to keep going while it hurts, "for extended periods of time," without reopening the question of whether the pursuit was worth beginning.

**Why these are hard:** they are anti-human. "We as humans want new stuff. We want it now, and we want it to be easy. And those three things are actually the antithesis of what is required in order to be successful in business."

**The line that carries the whole idea:** "A focused fool will beat a distracted genius."

### The three decision questions

These are the operational part of the source. Each converts a trait into something answerable in the moment.

1. **The dangerous-competitor question (for focus).** "If I were the worst or the most dangerous competitor, somebody who I would not want to compete against, what would that person do in my specific situation?" He attributes the effect to **Solomon's paradox** — we give better advice than we take, because advice to someone else carries no emotional tie. Note: he names the concept from memory mid-sentence; the underlying research is not cited in the video.

2. **The emotion question (for frustration tolerance).** "If I feel an emotion, is the action I'm taking to try and make this emotion go away, or is it what my business needs me to do? And those are often not the same thing."

   The trap he names is specific and worth quoting in full, because taking action genuinely does feel better while being wrong: "You taking action oftentimes does alleviate anxiety, but it doesn't mean that the action was still correct." What it looks like from outside is whiplash — "they said it was this priority last week, and then the next week it's this next priority" — which reads to customers as confused messaging and standing for nothing.

   His answer: "More often than not, what you need to do is keep doing what you were doing, and just let this emotion pass. Because emotions are like the weather. They come and go."

3. **The new-opportunity question (for focus, applied to expansion).** "Is there anything that logically stops my existing business from doing twice as much or five times as much or ten times as much, besides the fact that it's hard?"

   The failure mode underneath it: "I somehow think this other thing is easier because I haven't done it yet, but I promise you, it will also be hard. And so it's just unknown hard versus known hard." His rule: **take known hard.**

### Surface area of thinking

His model of what patience actually buys you, and the most transferable idea in the video.

> "How much time have I given this idea? How much air? How many coats of paint have I put over this problem, where I walk away and I come back and look at the paint, make adjustments, go back, look at the paint."

The mechanism: over time you return to the same problem in different moods, after different conversations, with different comparisons available. "The more diverse the lenses that you look at a solution through, the more complete and elegant the solution will come. And that only comes from having that many repetitions over periods of time on a specific problem."

This reframes patience from waiting into accumulating passes. It also means a problem you switched away from at pass two never gets the pass-five version of the answer, which is the concrete cost of switching.

### The irony worth keeping

And so you have to be able to stick with things for an extended period of time and not start new projects, so you can see things through. Because fundamentally, all great things come from doing more work in a very narrow place than other people are willing to do."

The single most-rewatched moment in a video about not letting new inputs redirect you is the moment describing a person being redirected by a new input. Useful to say out loud to anyone who has just watched it and wants to redesign their system in response.

## How this folds into the skill

- Adds the **switching filter** (SKILL.md) — a gate for new projects, distinct from the five MAP filters, which only cut a list of actions inside an already-chosen result.
- Adds the **commitment horizon** as an explicit RPM output: how long the result stays locked before it may be reconsidered.
- Supplies the diagnostic split coaches should run before prescribing: is this person failing to *choose* correctly, or failing to *stay* on a choice? The rest of this skill treats every problem as the first one.

## What this source does NOT establish

- No evidence, sample, or study is offered. The population is self-selected: founders who visit Acquisition.com headquarters, stated as roughly $1M to $50M in revenue.
- The claim that these traits are causal rather than correlated with success is asserted, not shown.
- "Solomon's paradox" is named from memory and not cited.
- The framing is business-specific throughout. Its application to personal goals, school, or health is an extension, not something the source claims.
