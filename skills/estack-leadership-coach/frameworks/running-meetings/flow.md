# Running great team meetings flow (Omission 4)

<primary_outcome>
The user leaves with a written **Meeting Redesign**: the meeting's real purpose, what gets cut, how conflict and decisions get forced into the room, and the specific change in how the user shows up. If the user treats meetings as drudgery to get through, the surgery reframe gets delivered before the redesign.
</primary_outcome>

This flow runs when the user's meetings are boring, decision-free, or dreaded — theirs or the ones they run. Grounded in [Lencioni — *The Motive*](../../references/lencioni_the-motive.md) (Omission 4) and [Doerr — *Measure What Matters*](../../references/doerr_measure-what-matters.md) (decisions and priorities).

---

## When to run this flow

- "My staff meeting is boring / a status readout / a waste of time"
- "We never actually decide anything in meetings"
- "I dread my own meetings" / "I check out during them"
- "Should we just cancel meetings?"
- The motive gate flagged the user offloading or avoiding their meetings

---

## The frame (grounded in *The Motive*)

Lencioni treats meetings as central, not administrative: *"Meetings are the setting, the arena, the moment when the most important discussions and decisions take place."* The reframe that reorients a leader who dreads them:

> *"A leader seeing his or her meetings as drudgery would be like a doctor viewing surgery that way. Or a teacher thinking about class lectures that way. Or a quarterback seeing games that way."*

The best place to observe a leader *is* a meeting — it's where they do their most visible, highest-leverage work. Two consequences of tolerating bad meetings: (1) *"it leads to bad decision-making"* — a disengaged room makes worse calls; and (2) the ceiling effect — **"What is tolerated at the top of a company is often the ceiling of what can be expected deeper within it."** The CEO who runs dull meetings licenses every manager below to do the same.

The most common failure isn't a bad agenda template — it's the leader who has emotionally checked out, delegated the meeting to HR, or lets the room stay in artificial harmony. Good meetings have real conflict (the important disagreements surfaced and worked, not buried) and end in clear decisions. (Lencioni's *Death by Meeting* — matching meeting type to purpose so the tactical doesn't crowd out the strategic — is the fuller model; cite it as his related work, don't fabricate a reference file.)

## The flow (run the moves in order)

### Move 1 — Pinpoint the meeting and the failure (Listen)

> **I need two answers:**
> 1. Which specific meeting are we fixing, and what's it supposed to accomplish?
> 2. When it's at its worst, what's actually wrong — boring, no decisions, no conflict, wrong people, or you're checked out?

### Move 2 — The surgery reframe and the mirror (Educate + Apply)

If the user frames the meeting as a chore, deliver the reframe: this is your operating room. If you're bored in it, the room is bored, and the decisions made there are worse for it — and every manager below you is watching what you tolerate.

Then the honest mirror:

> **Question:** Be straight — how present are you in this meeting? Do you check out, look at your phone, let it run on autopilot?

A leader's disengagement is usually the root cause, not the agenda. Name it if it's there.

**The self-check (ask these verbatim — a "yes" is the tell).** Lencioni closes this omission with two reflection questions. Ask them straight, and hold the user to the answer:

> **Ask yourself:**
> - *"Do you complain about your own meetings being boring or ineffective, and do you long for the end of them?"*
> - *"Do you allow your people, and yourself, to check out during those meetings, or perhaps skip them from time to time for 'more important' work?"*

A "yes" to either means, in Lencioni's words, *"you may have a problem with your leadership motive."* This is the same drudgery the surgery reframe targets — a "yes" is the leader treating their operating room as a chore. Name it, then redesign.

### Move 3 — Redesign for conflict and decisions (Apply + Execute)

Rebuild the meeting around three moves:

- **Purpose and cut.** Restate what the meeting is *for*, and cut everything that isn't that — especially status readouts that could be a written update. Protect the room for discussion and decisions.
- **Mine for conflict.** Name how the user will surface the important disagreements instead of letting the room stay politely quiet. (Call on the skeptic; put the real trade-off on the table; ask "who disagrees?")
- **Force the decision.** Each key item ends with a named decision and owner — not "let's keep thinking about it." Doerr's discipline: decisions and priorities, made explicit.

### Move 4 — The leader's behavior change (Execute)

Land the one thing the user will do differently in the very next instance of this meeting — full presence, opening with the real question, refusing to let it end without decisions. Capture it. The redesign fails if the leader shows up the same way.

## Pre-empted shortcuts

- **Don't just fix the agenda.** A tighter agenda with a checked-out leader is still a dead meeting. The leader's presence is usually the root — surface it.
- **Don't leave conflict out.** A meeting with no disagreement isn't harmony, it's avoidance, and it produces worse decisions. Build in the move that surfaces it.
- **Don't end items without decisions.** "Let's revisit this" is the failure mode. Every key item gets a decision and an owner.
- **Don't accept "cancel it" as the fix** unless the meeting genuinely has no purpose. Usually the meeting matters and the leader checked out of it.
