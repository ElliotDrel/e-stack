# Communicating constantly and repetitively flow (Omission 5)

<primary_outcome>
The user leaves with a written **Communication Plan**: the one core message that has to land, a repetition schedule across channels and occasions built on the rule of seven, and the user's commitment to being the Chief Reminding Officer of that message — including how they'll keep it fresh without abandoning it. If the user thinks they've already communicated enough, the rule-of-seven reframe gets delivered first.
</primary_outcome>

This flow runs when a message isn't landing — a strategy, a change, a value — and the user is frustrated their people "didn't get it," or bored of repeating themselves. Grounded in [Lencioni — *The Motive*](../../references/lencioni_the-motive.md) (Omission 5) and [Doerr — *Measure What Matters*](../../references/doerr_measure-what-matters.md) (a single clear priority to communicate).

**Scope seam:** this flow is for a message that has to reach and stick with *many people over time*. If the "message" is the why behind one specific handoff to one person, that's enrollment — `../delegation/phases/3-enrollment.md` handles it.

---

## When to run this flow

- "I announced the strategy and people still don't get it"
- "I've already said this — why do I have to keep repeating myself?"
- "Do I sound like a broken record?"
- Rolling out a change, a new priority, or a cultural value
- The motive gate flagged the user treating communication as one-and-done

---

## The frame (grounded in *The Motive*)

Most leaders don't hate communicating — they *"greatly underestimate the amount of communication that is necessary,"* and think they've done the job long before anyone has internalized it. Lencioni's anecdote: a CEO who thought he'd been saying "we have to save this company" for a year; his own head of HR had never heard it.

The load-bearing facts:

- **The rule of seven.** *"Employees have to hear a message seven times before they believe executives are serious about it."* Until then they discount it as corporate speak.
- **No one leaves from over-communication.** *"No reasonable human being has ever left a company because management communicated too much."* The risk is entirely on the under-communication side.
- **It's emotional, not transactional.** *"The reason a CEO communicates… is to ensure that people are aligned with and have bought into what is going on."* It's a behavioral process, not the dissemination of information.
- **The CRO.** The best leaders act as **Chief Reminding Officers** — they repeat the fundamental messages *"again and again and again,"* finding fresh ways to say them but never abandoning them (Gary Kelly at Southwest; Mulally at Ford refusing to swap "the plan" for a new flavor of the month).

The reward-centered tell: the leader gets *bored* with their own message and goes looking for new material. *"The purpose of communication is not their own entertainment."* Boredom is the trap — the moment the leader is sick of the message is roughly the moment employees are starting to hear it.

## The flow (run the moves in order)

### Move 1 — Isolate the one message (Listen)

> **I need two answers:**
> 1. What's the single most important message you need people to internalize right now — in one sentence?
> 2. Who has to believe it, and how do you know they don't yet?

If the user offers five messages, push to one. A diluted message repeated is still diluted. (Doerr's discipline: one clear priority beats a list.)

### Move 2 — The rule-of-seven reframe (Educate + Apply)

Whatever the user's sense of "I've said it," recalibrate: seven times before people believe you're serious, and you're almost certainly not close. If they're worried about sounding repetitive, deliver the counter: no one ever quit over too much clarity, and boredom with your own message is the tell, not the finish line.

**The self-check (ask these verbatim — a "yes" is the tell).** Lencioni closes this omission with two reflection questions. Ask them straight, and hold the user to the answer:

> **Ask yourself:**
> - *"Do you resent having to repeat yourself, complaining that your employees don't listen?"*
> - *"Do you look for new messages and ideas to communicate because you get bored saying the same things again and again?"*

A "yes" to either means, in Lencioni's words, *"your motive for leadership may not be quite right."* The second question is the boredom trap named directly — if the user is hunting for fresh material, that's the tell, not creativity. Name it, then build the plan that keeps the *same* message alive.

### Move 3 — Build the repetition plan (Apply + Execute)

Design the message hitting people repeatedly across varied surfaces so it doesn't read as a single rote loop. Land specifics across two axes:

- **Channels:** all-hands, 1:1s, written note/newsletter, onboarding, Slack, informal moments. Pick several.
- **Occasions:** the natural moments over the next quarter where this message can attach — decisions, wins, setbacks, reviews. The message rides real events, not just scheduled speeches.

Aim for the message reaching the key audience well past seven times over the coming weeks, in different forms.

### Move 4 — The CRO commitment and staying fresh (Execute)

Two captures:

- The user's explicit commitment to be the Chief Reminding Officer of this message — to keep saying it after they're bored of it.
- How they'll keep it *creative* — new stories, new framings, tied to current events — so repetition doesn't become noise. Same message, fresh clothes.

## Pre-empted shortcuts

- **Don't let the user communicate five things.** A repeated diluted message is still diluted. Force it to one.
- **Don't validate "I've already said it."** The rule of seven is the coaching — recalibrate what "enough" means.
- **Don't let boredom drive new material.** The instant the leader wants fresh content is when employees are just starting to hear it. Same message, fresh delivery — not a new message.
- **Don't build a one-time announcement plan.** A single all-hands is not communication; it's a starting gun. The plan spans channels and weeks.
