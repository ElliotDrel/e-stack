# Developing the leadership team flow (Omission 1)

<primary_outcome>
The user leaves with a written **Team Development Plan**: the one team-health gap they'll work on first (interpersonal dynamics, honesty/conflict, or peer accountability), the specific leader-owned intervention to close it (not something handed to HR), and a scheduled first move. If the user is trying to outsource team-building, that gets named before the plan is built.
</primary_outcome>

This flow runs when the user wants their leadership team to work better together — or when the motive gate caught them trying to hand team-building to HR or an offsite facilitator. Grounded in [Lencioni — *The Motive*](../../references/lencioni_the-motive.md) (Omission 1) and [Deci & Ryan — Self-Determination Theory](../../references/deci-ryan_self-determination-theory.md).

---

## When to run this flow

- "My team doesn't gel" / "there's no trust" / "they don't push on each other"
- "Should HR run our offsite?" / "I brought in a facilitator and nothing changed"
- The team avoids conflict, or has conflict that turns political
- People don't hold each other accountable — everything routes through the user
- The motive gate flagged an attempt to delegate team development

---

## The frame (grounded in *The Motive*)

Lencioni's Omission 1: developing the leadership team is *"often delegated, and sometimes even abdicated completely."* The most common form is handing it to HR — and *"this does not work. If people on a leadership team don't believe that the leader sees team development as one of his or her most critical roles, they're not going to take it seriously."* Team development is not trust-falls; it is four concrete things:

1. **Improving interpersonal dynamics** — how team members actually interact.
2. **Facilitating honesty and conflict** — getting the team to argue well, out loud.
3. **Enforcing peer accountability** — members calling each other out, not routing everything through the leader.
4. **Navigating uncomfortable conversations** — behavior, politics, the things everyone avoids.

Why leaders abdicate it: they don't see it as central (teamwork feels "soft" next to finance/strategy), and effective team-building *"always involves emotional and uncomfortable conversations"* they'd rather dodge. Lencioni's counter: a healthy team is *"the practical, tangible competitive advantage"* that makes finance, strategy, and marketing work. (Lencioni's *Five Dysfunctions of a Team* — trust → conflict → commitment → accountability → results — is the fuller model behind these four; cite it as his related work, don't fabricate a reference file for it.)

The leader's job here is not to *run an exercise*. It's to own the hard part personally: model the vulnerability, name the elephant, hold the first uncomfortable conversation.

## The flow (run the moves in order)

### Move 1 — Name the gap, and check who's supposed to close it (Listen)

> **I need two answers:**
> 1. When your team is at its worst, what does that actually look like — in a specific recent moment?
> 2. Whose job have you been treating team development as — yours, HR's, a facilitator's, or no one's?

If the answer to (2) is anyone but the user, that's the first coaching moment. Name it: team development that the team doesn't see the leader owning won't land. The plan you build has to be leader-owned.

### Move 2 — Locate the layer (Educate + Apply)

Map the moment from Move 1 to one of the four layers. Ask which resonates most:

- **Trust / interpersonal dynamics** — people are guarded, don't admit weakness, read each other wrong.
- **Conflict / honesty** — disagreements happen in the hallway, not the room; artificial harmony in meetings.
- **Peer accountability** — nobody calls anybody out; the user is the only enforcer.
- **Uncomfortable behavior / politics** — a specific person or dynamic everyone tiptoes around.

Teach the dependency briefly: without trust, there's no real conflict; without conflict, no commitment; without commitment, no peer accountability. Start at the lowest broken layer — fixing accountability while trust is absent doesn't hold.

**The self-check (ask these verbatim — a "yes" is the tell).** Lencioni closes this omission with two reflection questions. Ask them straight, and hold the user to the answer:

> **Ask yourself:**
> - *"Do you feel that spending time developing your team members' interpersonal dynamics is superfluous or a waste of time?"*
> - *"Do you organize 'team-building' activities for your team that are fun but that largely ignore uncomfortable conversations about their collective behaviors?"*

A "yes" to either is a sign the motive is off. Call it out plainly — Lencioni's own words: you *"have a serious decision to make. You can either rethink your attitude about team-building and embrace it as the practical, indispensable discipline that it is, or you can accept that the organization you lead will never come close to reaching its full potential."* Don't let a "yes" slide past — name it, then build the plan that acts on the other choice.

### Move 3 — Pick the leader-owned intervention (Apply)

For the chosen layer, land on one concrete move the *user personally* makes. Examples to adapt:

- Trust → the leader goes first: shares a real weakness/mistake in the next team meeting to make it safe.
- Conflict → the leader mines for the buried disagreement in the room and names it, instead of letting it stay in the hallway.
- Accountability → the leader stops being the router: when someone brings a peer problem, the leader sends it back ("have you told them?").
- Behavior/politics → the leader books the difficult conversation (route to `../difficult-conversations/flow.md` to script it).

Reject "schedule a teambuilding day" as the whole answer. An offsite can help, but only if the leader carries the uncomfortable part.

### Move 4 — Schedule the first move and define how the leader shows up (Execute)

Nail down when the first intervention happens and what the user will actually do/say. Capture it into the plan. A plan with no date and no leader behavior is not output.

## Pre-empted shortcuts

- **Don't let the plan be "hire a facilitator."** A facilitator can assist, but if the leader outsources the emotional core, the team learns it's not really a priority. The user owns the hard part.
- **Don't skip layers.** Enforcing accountability on a team with no trust produces resentment, not results. Start at the lowest broken layer.
- **Don't accept "we need better communication" as the gap.** Push to a specific recent moment where the team was at its worst — the gap lives there.
- **Don't confuse fun with development.** A ropes course that never touches the uncomfortable behavior is the exact avoidance Lencioni names.
